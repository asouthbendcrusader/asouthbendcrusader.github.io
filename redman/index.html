Public Court Records • File No. 5

THE BILL REDMAN FILE

Sheriff of St. Joseph County, Indiana — 2019 to 2026
Compiled and published by A South Bend Crusader
Follow A South Bend Crusader

This file is built from the government's own records and the public paper of a thirty-two-year career: court opinions and dockets, commissioners' minutes and settlement packets, state audits, the County Auditor's own fund history, official election results, campaign filings, and one county attorney's email, read in full. Where a record makes a point, this file prints the record. Where a claim is an allegation, this file labels it an allegation, every time. Where the record has not been produced yet, this file says so and names the pull that settles it.

Three boundaries govern everything below. A settlement is not a finding of liability, and a judge letting a lawsuit proceed is not a verdict — the settlements in §04 are printed with the denials that came with them. The deaths in §03 are the deaths on his watch, from the day he took the oath; the one earlier death this file discusses is fenced off as earlier, in its own box, because it happened ten months before he was sheriff. And this file prints what the record shows in his favor, at full weight, in its own section (§13): two election wins on verified numbers, a federal drug-trafficking designation his county won on his watch, eighteen years teaching in classrooms, and fourteen years of state audits that never found a sheriff's fund out of line.

§01 — The Man and the Office

William "Bill" Redman has been Sheriff of St. Joseph County since January 1, 2019. The sheriff's office is the St. Joseph County Police Department, headquartered at 401 West Sample Street in South Bend, and Indiana law charges the sheriff to "take care of the county jail and the prisoners there." The St. Joseph County Jail, at the same address, is rated for 829 inmates on the county's own Community Corrections page. His second and final term ends December 31, 2026 — Indiana's term limit for sheriffs is why there is a race to succeed him at all.

His own answers to the South Bend Tribune and League of Women Voters questionnaire (published October 24, 2026) give his credentials: graduate of Riley High School; attended Bethel College; graduate of the Indiana Law Enforcement Academy, class 95-120; graduate of the FBI LEEDS Academy, class 79. His campaign's summary of the career is "32 years of service to St. Joseph County," and the record below is that career: patrol, the DARE classroom, the department's public voice, and then eight years running the jail.

In 2026 he asked the voters to promote him: Democratic candidate for County Commissioner, District 1, against the incumbent Republican, Carl Baxmeyer — the only contested commissioner seat on the ballot, with control of the three-member board riding on it. The race and its result are at §12.

§02 — The Road to the Sheriff's Office

1994. Redman joined the St. Joseph County Police Department as a Special Deputy. 1997: he became a DARE officer, beginning eighteen years teaching drug-resistance classes in Penn-Harris-Madison schools — the role that made his name in the county, and the role at the centre of §10. 2008: he became the department's public information officer, its public voice — the man who issued its statements, including, in February 2018, its statement on a death this file returns to in §03.

His campaign biography adds one more early entry: a temporary assignment as assistant chief from July through December 2010, with daily oversight of the jail. That line matters at §11, where audit findings from 2011 and 2012 overlap it — partially, and with no document naming who owned what.

May 2018, the Democratic primary. Redman 9,059. Tim Corbett 9,043. Thompson 4,031. Sixteen votes — verified from the official results — separated the sheriff from the man who finished second. He won the general election that November and took the oath on January 1, 2019. The transition was not smooth on paper: outgoing Sheriff Mike Grzegorek publicly criticized Redman's cooperation following a promotion while backing a rival, and Redman disputed the criticism, saying he had not previously been told of those concerns. That dispute is documented from both sides, and this file prints it as exactly that.

November 2022, the general election. Redman 38,656 votes, 54.21 percent; McGowan 32,646, 45.79 percent — verified. Term-limited out of a third run, he filed for county commissioner on January 29, 2026.

Before his tenure — kept separate on purpose

Raymond McCarty, 52, died in the same jail's medical unit on February 25, 2018 — ten months before Redman took office, under Sheriff Mike Grzegorek. A federal lawsuit alleged McCarty told staff he took medications for depression, blood pressure and back pain, including an opioid, and did not receive them, and that he died in withdrawal. Redman's documented connection is narrow and this file keeps it narrow: he was the department's public information officer, and he issued its statement saying it took the matter "very seriously." He was not the sheriff. The lawsuit's outcome has never been verified — no public record of a settlement, verdict, or dismissal has been found. The sequence is the reason it appears here at all: three years before William Anderson died in withdrawal on Redman's watch, another man's federal lawsuit alleged the same failure in the same jail — and the man who answered for the department that day is the man who was running it when it happened again.

§04 — The Settlements: $822,500

Adding the seven approved or reported settlement amounts below gives $822,500. That is a calculation from the listed amounts, not an audited total; insurance contributions, actual disbursements, and defense fees have never been separately accounted. And the standing rule of this file applies to every line: a settlement does not automatically establish liability, and several of these were paid over express denials of liability.

William Anderson — $695,000

Anderson entered the jail on April 6, 2021, arrested by State Police on drug charges. According to the allegations recounted in U.S. District Judge Damon R. Leichty's August 2023 opinion, intake documented addiction to heroin and pills, blackouts, and visible withdrawal; a nurse requested medical observation and he was placed in a suicide-watch cell; jail policy required checks every fifteen minutes, and the complaint alleged those checks were missed on April 7 and April 8; at 9:21 p.m. on his first evening an employee recorded that he was "detoxing and throwing up" and "just wants to go to the hospital." He stayed in his cell. The record also says he refused anti-nausea injections.

On April 8 he was found face down, unresponsive, without a pulse and not breathing. Reporting on the complaint (Redress South Bend, October 10, 2026) says the firefighters who responded noted he was severely malnourished and bruised in multiple stages of healing; his hospital weight was recorded as 89 pounds. Life support was removed on April 11, 2021. The postmortem found he died of complications of drug withdrawal, including dehydration. Judge Leichty allowed the claim against the nurse to proceed — the observation checks were "plausibly critical" — and dismissed the doctor. That was a ruling that the allegations were legally sufficient to move forward. It was not a verdict.

On May 26, 2026, the county commissioners approved a $695,000 settlement, 3–0: $445,000 to the estate and its lawyers, $250,000 toward an annuity for Anderson's son. The defendants denied liability. The minutes record county attorney Peter Agostino describing potential exposure "in the millions" after three mediation sessions. The withdrawal protocols, observation logs, staffing assignments, training records, and corrective-action findings that would show what failed and what changed have not been produced.

Lynn Mahoney — a reported $50,000

In June 2019, jail nurse Lynn Mahoney posted criticism of a police encounter in Phoenix. The federal opinion describes the jail's warden showing the post to Redman, both finding it offensive, the warden requesting Mahoney's removal from the jail, and her employer then terminating her; the defendants also raised performance concerns. The court allowed her First Amendment retaliation and business-interference claims to proceed past summary judgment, and later reporting documented a $50,000 settlement in March 2022. That is not a finding that Redman personally fired her — her employer did the firing. It is a documented $50,000 cost of a dispute that began with a Facebook post criticizing police.

Legal publications — a reported $30,000

The Human Rights Defense Center sued over restrictions on publications mailed to inmates. In February 2023 the commissioners reportedly approved $30,000 to resolve the case; the agreement allowed legal and criminal-defense periodicals without a subscription and required removing staples instead of rejecting publications. Officials defended the earlier restrictions as protection against drugs entering through the mail, and denied liability.

Joshua E. Williams — $2,500, and a clause

Williams challenged restrictions on Muslim religious items and Friday prayer; some of his statutory religious-exercise claims survived a November 2022 ruling. The commissioners' January 10, 2023 meeting packet contains a $2,500 settlement agreement, approved 3–0 — and its paragraph 14 restricts Williams from disclosing the agreement's terms and existence, prescribing the response: "The matter has been resolved." The agreement itself sits in a public packet. A public jail, public money, and paperwork telling the man what he was allowed to say about it: whether such a clause belongs in a public settlement is a transparency question this file asks outright.

Held past their release dates — $20,000 and $12,500

William Davis Jr. received $20,000 in 2021 after spending approximately sixty extra days in jail in 2019. Officials attributed the error to release-date software; the department's attorney acknowledged another inmate had been held two additional days and that earlier cases could theoretically have been affected. No complete count of everyone affected has ever been produced. Ian McQueen received $12,500 after being wrongfully detained for 43 days amid overlapping proceedings in two counties — the county's attorney blamed communication between court systems and expressly disputed sheriff or jail responsibility.

Carlton Collins — $12,500, then a lawsuit

The January 27, 2026 commissioners' packet contains a $12,500 agreement with Carlton Alphonzo Collins over incarceration from October 2023 through May 31, 2024 — a delayed release involving two people with the same name and uncertainty about a hold — approved 3–0, payment due within thirty days, and Collins's promise not to sue conditioned on receiving payment. On May 29, 2026, Collins filed a federal civil-rights lawsuit naming Redman, Russell Olmstead, other defendants, and the county. The available docket does not explain how the lawsuit relates to the agreement, and it does not establish that the county failed to pay. A settlement with a promise not to sue, followed four months later by a lawsuit anyway: the complaint, the executed agreement, and the payment records that explain that sequence have not been produced.

Dan Bodle — a plausible claim, an unverified ending

Officer Dan Bodle sued Redman and the department's attorney, alleging he was told — incorrectly — that no further disability leave was available, retired on October 30, 2020, and then learned the merit rules potentially allowed up to twelve months at full pay and twelve more at half. On April 20, 2022, Judge Leichty denied the motion to dismiss: the allegations supported a plausible procedural-due-process claim. Plausible is the whole of it — the opinion did not establish deliberate deception, and the case's final outcome has not been verified.

§05 — Other Lawsuits: Allegations, Not Findings

Three more cases belong in the accounting precisely because of what they do and do not show. In Wilkins, an amputee alleged inadequate medication for phantom-limb pain and spasms, confinement in medical isolation for roughly twenty-three hours a day, and inaccessible conditions; a February 2024 order let specified claims against medical personnel and the warden proceed and dismissed Redman, because the pleading did not plausibly establish his personal involvement. In Peavy, a May 2023 screening order likewise let claims against staff proceed while dismissing Redman. Burton (sanitation and food allegations, inadequately pleaded) and Hope (an alleged release delay, an improper defendant named) both ended the reviewed stage with leave to amend. These are allegations about the operation he oversees. They are not findings, and counting lawsuit titles alone would not establish misconduct — which is why this file prints the dismissals of Redman himself in the same breath as the claims.

§06 — The Bus Crash: A Pursuit, a County Line, and a Log Entry

On May 8, 2025, a St. Joseph County pursuit ended at the LaPorte County line, and a box truck hauling Amazon freight crashed into a New Prairie school bus. The truck driver, an Illinois man, pleaded guilty to two felonies for driving with drugs in his system and was sentenced to nine years in April 2026. A boy on the bus suffered a traumatic brain injury; his family's filings say he spent 33 days at Memorial Hospital and 92 days at a rehabilitation hospital, and that his medical bills have passed $1.25 million. His father was driving the bus and broke his sternum and ribs.

The Bradshaw family's lawsuit — reported in July 2026 and covered by Redress South Bend — does not fault the deputies for ending the pursuit. It alleges they failed to notify LaPorte County when they ended it, denying officers there the chance to intervene. According to Redress South Bend's reporting on the suit, the lawsuit quotes LaPorte County's own dispatch records:

"LP CO WAS NOT NOTIFIED OF PURSUIT WITH ST JOE"
LaPorte County dispatch record, as quoted in the Bradshaw family's lawsuit and reported by Redress South Bend. The underlying dispatch logs themselves have not been produced.

Three related cases — Shail, J.V., and Henrich — were returned to state court in June 2026 because the federal court lacked jurisdiction; that decision decided nothing about liability. A further fight is scheduled first: on November 19, 2026, a LaPorte County judge hears the dispute over the proper defendant. The county's lawyers argue the family sued the wrong government — the sheriff holds an office created by the Indiana Constitution, and the county "does not have any control over the acts of the sheriff." If the judge agrees the sheriff is the proper defendant, the family wants to add "Sheriff William Redman, in his official capacity" to the lawsuit. The operational questions underneath are concrete and still unanswered on produced records: what did dispatch know, what was communicated across the county line, and what did the pursuit policy require? The radio traffic, dispatch logs, and written policy are the records that settle them.

§07 — The Deputies Who Let a Driver Go

On February 5, 2021, Corporals Brad Bauters (hired 2014) and James Hart (hired 2013) answered a slide-off crash involving Stephen Stopczynski, 32, of North Liberty, whom a 911 caller said appeared intoxicated. As reported by the South Bend Tribune from the department's own statement and internal-investigation findings, the two deputies did not arrest him, did not run his license — which would have shown an outstanding LaPorte County felony warrant — accepted his refusal of a sobriety test, sent him off with a ride, and then reported and told investigators he had not appeared intoxicated. About four and a half hours later, Stopczynski, driving the same car with its headlights off, crossed the median and struck a family's SUV head-on. He was killed, with a blood-alcohol level of 0.15. Six people were seriously injured, including 13-year-old Giuliana Mendez, who suffered a spinal injury and partial paralysis.

Redman filed termination charges against both men with the county police merit board on May 21, 2021, and both sat on unpaid leave while hearings were set. They were never formally terminated: on July 23, 2021, both resigned, Redman withdrew the charges due to the resignations, and the proceedings ceased with no adjudicated finding. Every "fired deputies" headline on this story is shorthand. The formal record is a resignation ahead of a hearing — and the gap matters, because neither man appears on the Indiana Law Enforcement Academy's Certificate Decertification List (current as of June 15, 2026): there is no state bar to either of them wearing an Indiana badge again. No criminal charges or federal suit were found. The discipline Redman's department initiated is documented; so is where it stopped.

§08 — The Jail and the Informant Scandal: What This File Does and Does Not Say

The county jail Redman runs was the housing venue for the jailhouse informants at the centre of St. Joseph County's informant scandal — men held in that jail whose testimony helped convict other men, and who later said, on the record, that they had lied. Terrance Peterson's recantation says he lied under oath in two county murder trials. Tyre Bradbury, convicted in one of those cases, pleaded to aggravated battery on March 14, 2024 and was released on time served — a plea, not an exoneration. Shyheem Jones's conviction was vacated and he is reported free. Jermaine Munn's post-conviction case, alleging coerced jail witnesses, remains pending, and the allegations in it are denied.

The boundaries of Redman's connection are exact, and this file holds them. The allegations reported by WNDU's "Trial by Snitch" (April 24, 2026) — a single source for the claims — name an investigator, Tim Corbett, and the then-prosecutor, Christopher Fronk. They do not name Redman. What the record shows about the sheriff's office is two things only: the informants were housed in the jail he runs, and no on-record response from the Sheriff — or from Fronk, Corbett, or the South Bend police — was found when the reporting was assembled. What benefits the informants received has never been reported, and whether the sheriff's office played any role in informant placement, movement, or benefits inside the jail is not found in any record in this file. Those are open questions. They are printed as questions, and as nothing more.

§09 — Running the Department

Staffing. April 2024 reporting described the jail as 59 officers short. Authorized correction-officer strength in the 2018–19 budget was roughly 145 to 146. The 2024 figure is a historical snapshot; it does not establish staffing today. The dated numbers the public still does not have: authorized versus filled positions, vacancies by shift, overtime, turnover, and compliance with the observation schedules that §04 shows being missed.

The April 2026 outbreak. On April 29, 2026, twenty-three inmates developed similar symptoms, and jail medical staff suspected foodborne Salmonella (ABC57; WNDU). Redman said most had recovered, and described an earlier kitchen inspection that found no critical violations but identified standing water and mold on cooler racks, which he said were corrected. Those conditions are not established as the outbreak's cause, and testing and the source were unresolved in the reporting. The inspection report, laboratory results, outbreak investigation, food-service contract, and corrective actions are the records that settle it.

Inspection paper. State jail-inspection reports for the adult jail during Redman's tenure were not found in this file's sweep. Indiana's Department of Correction inspects county jails annually; the reports are public records on request but are not posted by the state, and none appear on the county's site. (Inspection reports for the county's juvenile facility — a Probate Court operation, not the sheriff's jail — do circulate; they are a different facility and prove nothing here either way.)

Cameras. The department uses Flock license-plate cameras — 148 plate readers documented county-wide, 139 of them Flock (Atlas of Surveillance / EFF). Indiana sets no statutory limit on the system; the county's own retention specifics are unverified.

The suspensions of his own deputy. Rod Laureys, a Redman deputy running as the Republican candidate to succeed him as sheriff, was suspended twice in 2026 by Redman's department (South Bend Tribune, July 27, 2026): three days without pay in January for failing to file the department letter announcing his candidacy — Laureys's words: he "dropped the ball" — and six days without pay in July for disobeying orders to return to Indiana during a May 11 pursuit that crossed into Niles, Michigan. Laureys calls the second suspension political retribution and says he had never been reprimanded in thirty years before this year. The suspensions are documented; the retribution claim is his characterization, printed as his. Former Sheriff Joe Speybroeck endorsed Laureys for sheriff, crossing party lines (Redress South Bend, August 11, 2026).

The immigration lawsuit — described accurately. Indiana's attorney general sued Redman over alleged restrictions on cooperation with federal immigration enforcement. The trial court dismissed the case in October 2025 — the judge finding, in the words quoted in reporting of the order, "almost a total dearth of operative facts plead." On August 19, 2026, the Indiana Court of Appeals dismissed the appeal as moot after the legislature changed the statute, and directed the trial judgment vacated. No court ruled that Redman violated the law. The dismissal was about mootness, not the merits, and this file prints it as exactly that.

§10 — The DARE Money

Bill Redman was the department's DARE officer for eighteen years, from 1997, teaching in Penn-Harris-Madison schools. DARE ran on donations — money handed over at fundraisers by people who trusted that a deputy teaching their kids was a cause worth giving to. In 2018, a reporter asked the county in writing where that money had been kept. The county attorney's answer, and Redman's answer, and the county's own books, agree on more than they fight about. This section prints all three, and then prints the one question they leave open.

The county attorney's email — read in full

On November 2, 2018, county attorney Eric Tamashasky answered by email. Three facts in it have never been disputed by anyone, including Redman: the government's DARE account was created in early 2012; "no records exist about DARE finances before 2012 that are in our custody"; and Bob Boits — a former chief then serving as assistant chief — had an account created through the Auditor's Office and advised Sgt. Redman to turn over all the DARE money he had, and Redman did so. The email does not say where the money sat before 2012, and it does not use the words "personal account." Anyone who tells you it does has not read it.

Redman's answer, in full

In 2022 the question reached print, and Redman's campaign answered it in a statement dated August 30, 2022. This file prints his answer the way it prints every answer: whole.

"No DARE funds were ever held in a personal account of Bill Redman… When DARE was started in 1996 Sheriff Joe Speybroeck, then Chief Bob Boits and department DARE lead officer John Radde opened an account at Key Bank for the DARE program. All monthly statements went to whomever was Chief… That Key Bank DARE account was used until 2012 when Chief Boits transferred all funds to a county financial account… The Key Bank account was closed by Chief Boits in 2012 when Mike Grzegorek was Sheriff and the Grzegorek administration did not retain any of those records."
Bill Redman campaign statement, August 30, 2022.

Read the two accounts side by side. They agree on everything except one thing: whose name was on the account the money sat in. His side says a department account at Key Bank. The other side says personal. The paper that would settle it is bank paper, and nobody has produced it. One tension sits inside his own version, and it is arithmetic, not accusation: the reporter who obtained the new county account's records described its opening day, February 28, 2012 — about $4,600 came in, a $2,000 grant reimbursement and six donations, and no item listed as a transfer from another account. If sixteen years of money had been moved over in one transfer, that is where it would show up. The same reporter's own bottom line was narrower than the fight around it: "I'm not saying that he did anything wrong… All I want is documentation." Redman did not appear on that broadcast.

The part that does not depend on the fight

Step back from whose name was on the account, because there is a fact underneath that no version of this story escapes — including Redman's own. By every account, donated DARE money sat outside the county's books from 1996 until early 2012. And the Indiana State Board of Accounts' rule for county money could not be plainer:

"At no time should there be any grant money or donations held separately by departments, those funds should be turned over to the treasurer for deposit and the auditor to create the appropriate grant fund."
Indiana State Board of Accounts, County Bulletin. Separate departmental ledgers are permitted for commissary, inmate trust, drug money, and trusts. Donations are expressly not on the list.

The county's own books back the timeline. The County Auditor's published history of Fund 9122, the Sheriff DARE Program, shows zero activity for 2011, 2012, 2013, and 2014; the 2011 state audit's list of the sheriff's accounts does not include a DARE fund at all. The first money the county's records show moving through the DARE fund is $30,130 in 2015 — and the fund's own first-year breakdown (2015 Annual Financial Report) is $18,732 in donations, $25 in other receipts, and $11,373.40 transferred in from another county fund whose name the report does not give. The fund peaked at $49,561 at the end of 2019 and held $30,876.12 on December 31, 2024; it is still budgeted in 2025 and 2026. Everything from 2015 forward is published and accounted for. Everything before it is a blank the county itself admits it cannot fill — and one detail still does not reconcile: the county attorney's email and the reporter's records put a government account in early 2012, while the Auditor's history shows the fund silent until 2015.

Two more fences on this section, stated plainly. A figure of roughly $100,000 in "unaccounted" DARE money circulates in this story; it is an extrapolation — roughly $8,000 a year multiplied across thirteen years — not a documented loss, and this file could not document it. And across fourteen years of state audits, 2011 through 2024, the State Board of Accounts issued no DARE finding and no sheriff-fund finding against this department; the only sheriff-fund item in those years is a 2012 finding that jail inmate-trust and commissary balances were overstated by $32,974, which is not DARE. No public-access complaint, no lawsuit over funds, and no statement on the account from Radde, Speybroeck, Grzegorek, or Thompson has been found.

On October 25, 2026, two public-records requests were filed to settle what the open record cannot: RDPR-2026-1678 to the Sheriff's Department (every DARE financial record from 1997 to 2012, any 2012 Boits directive, the county's complete 2018 file, and any record of where DARE donations were held before the county fund existed) and RDPR-2026-1679 to the County Auditor (the records creating Fund 9122, where its first deposit came from, and its earliest statements). As of this file's publication, no production from either request is in hand. When the county answers, the answer lands in this section, whichever way it cuts.

§11 — Money, Disclosures, and Earlier Paper

Donors and contracts — relationships worth examining, printed as exactly that. Redman's 2025 sheriff-campaign filing lists $500 from Anderson, Agostino & Keller (July 24, 2025). Lawyers from that firm represented defendants in the Anderson case, and the firm appears in the Williams settlement. That is a documented donor-and-representation connection; whether contributions and outcomes connect is what payment records, contracts, and bid files would have to answer, and no such connection is documented here. Other entries in his historical sheriff-committee filings: ASAP Towing, $500 in 2023, $1,500 in 2024, $2,000 in 2025; City Wide Towing, $1,000 in each of 2024 and 2025; "Combined Public Communication," as listed, $1,000 in 2024 and $400 in 2025; DLZ Indiana, $600 in 2023 and $1,000 in 2025 — DLZ describes historical work designing the county jail. The filings name Thomas Catanzarite as treasurer. A city towing contract is not automatically a sheriff's contract, and similar company names require verification before any donor is connected to any award; this file makes no such connection.

The commissioner campaign's money. His 2026 committee is the Committee to Elect Bill Redman for Commissioner. One itemized contribution was located in this file's sweep: the United Association Local #172 Political Action Committee gave $1,000 to Redman for Commissioner on May 21, 2026 (state filings as rendered by Transparency USA). County candidates file their full reports with the County Clerk; a complete accounting of the commissioner campaign was not found in this sweep, and a document missing from an online search does not prove a filing was never made. The clerk's filings are the settler.

The disclosure that does not reconcile — yet. Redman's Statement of Economic Interests, filed January 29, 2026 and covering 2025, marks the corporate officer/director question "N/A." His campaign advertises board service, and the CASIE Center's own page lists him in a board role — ex-officio — as does LOGAN's. The exact 2025 dates, the legal nature of those positions, and any amended filing are open questions. The filing and the service records need to be reconciled on the record; until they are, this file prints the question and nothing stronger.

Earlier-administration paper, with its limits printed on it. A 2011 sheriff audit identified late inmate-trust deposits and $261,055.35 in medical-service fees to be recovered, involving payments across 2008 through 2011; a 2012 audit again found late deposits. Neither audit names who was responsible. A class settlement over overdetention concerns 2009 and 2010. Redman's temporary assistant-chief assignment (§02) overlaps part of those years, and the approval and delegation records that would show who owned what have not been produced — so this file prints the overlap and the absence together, and draws no line between them. Separately, a 2023 county audit questioned $190,000 in settlement costs charged to federal recovery funds, including one $60,000 unallowable settlement payment; the finding names neither Redman nor the underlying cases, and the vouchers that would connect it to any jail settlement have not been produced.

§12 — The 2026 Race and the Result

Redman announced on January 29, 2026, in the language of a man closing one career and opening another: "As I prepare to retire from law enforcement, my commitment to this community is as strong as ever." His questionnaire answers put public safety first — "My 32 years as a police officer and Sheriff will provide our county leadership with the guidance to continue to put our community first" — with continued investment in police departments and expanded fire and emergency-management services. District 1 was the only contested seat on the Board of Commissioners; Republicans held the board 2–1, and this race decided control of it.

Result — reported, not certified (as of publication, election night, November 3, 2026)

The South Bend Tribune's election-night coverage reported at 9:40 PM that with 100% of votes counted, but results not yet officially certified, Democratic candidates appear to have won all of the countywide, county commissioner, and county council races in St. Joseph County, taking control of the Board of Commissioners by a 2–1 margin. District 1 was the only contested commissioner seat — which makes Redman the apparent winner over Carl Baxmeyer. Apparent is the word, and this file keeps it: race-specific vote totals for District 1 had not been published in any source this file could reach on election night, and no result is final until the county certifies it. The certified numbers, when the Clerk issues them, land here as a dated update — this file is a living file, and the result line will be completed from the county's own paper.

§13 — The Other Side of the Ledger

The standard of this series is that the file prints both ways, and Redman's credit side is not thin.

The voters, twice. He won the sheriff's office in 2018 — surviving a sixteen-vote primary — and was re-elected in 2022 with 54.21 percent in a contested general election. Whatever else the record holds, the county's voters read a version of it and chose him twice.

A federal designation on his watch. On September 22, 2025, the White House announced that St. Joseph County had been designated a High Intensity Drug Trafficking Area — one of two new county designations in the country that day. Redman's news release announcing it said the designation would bring federal funding, technology, training, and stronger coordination between local, state, and federal law enforcement; a rival candidate for sheriff credited Redman in his own questionnaire answers as a partner in the multi-year effort behind the application.

Eighteen years in the classroom. The DARE program he taught from 1997 ran in Penn-Harris-Madison schools and outlasted the money fight: graduations continued into the fall of 2024, and the fund behind it is still budgeted. The teaching itself is documented across eighteen years, and every dollar the county's own books show from 2015 forward is published and accounted for.

Fourteen clean audit years.
