PUBLIC COURT RECORDS

THE RYAN O’NEILL FILE

RYAN O’NEILL · SERGEANT, SOUTH BEND POLICE DEPARTMENT · THE ERIC LOGAN SHOOTING, JUNE 16, 2019
Resigned July 15, 2019 · The shooting ruled justified March 6, 2020 · Felony guilty plea September 8, 2020 · Federal suit decided for the defense, 2021–2022
What this file is. This file is built out of a prosecutor’s court petition, a special prosecutor’s determination, contemporaneous reporting of the plea and sentencing, two published federal court decisions, the state training board’s published lists, and the records demands listed at the end — and nothing else. Every factual statement here is drawn from the documents and reporting cited on this page. No photograph accompanies this file: no authentic photograph of O’Neill that could be credited to a source was located for this build, and this series does not publish pictures it cannot credit.
What this file does not claim. Read this first. A special prosecutor ruled the shooting of Eric Logan justified, and that ruling is printed in this file in his own words (Section 2). The material in Section 3 — the probable-cause affidavit — is allegation: it is what the state said in March 2020, and it is kept separate from what O’Neill actually admitted in his plea (Section 4). And this file concerns Ryan O’Neill, the former South Bend police sergeant — no other person of the same name.

WHO HE IS

Ryan O’Neill served the South Bend Police Department from 2000 to 2019 and was promoted to sergeant in 2015 (service record as reported; the Tribune described him in March 2020 as “a South Bend officer for 19 years”). He was 44 years old in the fall of 2020. In 2008 he was a patrol officer and a field training officer. By June 2019 he was a sergeant working uniform patrol in a marked squad car.

He is the same man in every record in this file: the sergeant named in Prosecutor Kenneth P. Cotter’s June 24, 2019 petition to the St. Joseph Circuit Court (Cause No. 71C01-1906-MCI), the defendant who pleaded guilty before Judge John Marnocha in 2020, and the named defendant in the federal case Estate of Eric Jack Logan v. City of South Bend, Indiana, and Ryan O’Neill, No. 3:19-cv-495. One man, one record, three courtrooms.

1 · JUNE 16, 2019 — THE SHOOTING OF ERIC LOGAN

In the early morning hours of June 16, 2019, a 911 caller reported a subject in dark clothing possibly “busting” out windows in the parking lot of the old high school — the Central High Apartments, at Colfax Avenue and William Street. Sgt. O’Neill, in uniform and a marked squad car, was dispatched at 3:27 AM. The core of what follows — the call, the dispatch, the two shots, the camera that stayed off — is stated in the prosecutor’s own petition to the court.

O’Neill came upon Eric Jack Logan, 54, leaning inside a parked car. Logan approached him holding a knife — described in the prosecutor’s account as a six-to-eight-inch knife raised over his head — and refused orders to drop it. O’Neill fired twice. One shot struck Logan in the upper abdomen; the other struck a car door.

His body camera was not on. The petition states it in plain words: “Sgt. O’Neill did not activate his body camera before this shooting.” His dash camera did not record either; the department said the body camera did not start on its own because his emergency lights were not activated. Cameras in a nearby building had been disconnected for at least five years. No video of the shooting exists.

The special prosecutor’s report, released the following March, filled in the physical record: the knife had been taken from one of the cars broken into that night — seven break-ins in a two-block radius; Logan’s blood-alcohol level measured 0.143, with cocaine and THC present; O’Neill had a knife abrasion on his left forearm; and O’Neill switched his body camera on after the shooting, not before it.

One point in the record contradicts itself, and this file prints it as a contradiction. Whether Logan threw the knife before or after the shots depends on which of O’Neill’s statements is read: his earliest statement at the scene suggested the throw came first; in his recorded interview with Metro Homicide investigators Brian Cook and Commander Mike Grzegorek, taken about four hours after the shooting, he placed the throw just after. His words for the shooting itself were these:

“I fired two quick shots.” — Sgt. Ryan O’Neill, recorded Metro Homicide interview, June 16, 2019 (as reported by the South Bend Tribune from the interview video)

What happened next is the point where this file meets another one. Sgt. Aaron Knepper, one of the officers who responded to the scene, proposed the “load-and-go”: put Logan in a police car and drive him to Memorial Hospital instead of waiting for an ambulance. O’Neill agreed. Logan was taken to Memorial in a police car, and he died at the hospital. Knepper was not a defendant in the family’s lawsuit and is not accused of any act in the encounter; his own file in this series carries his record.

O’Neill was placed on administrative leave. Under the county’s protocol, the County Metro Homicide Unit investigated the shooting.

2 · THE SPECIAL PROSECUTOR, THE RESIGNATION, AND THE CLEARANCE

On June 24, 2019, St. Joseph County Prosecutor Kenneth P. Cotter asked the St. Joseph Circuit Court to appoint a special prosecutor. His reason was a conflict inside his own office: his chief investigator, Dave Newton, had written a 2008 report about O’Neill — the report at the center of Section 6 of this file. On July 3, 2019, Circuit Judge John Broden appointed Ric Hertel, the prosecuting attorney of Ripley County, to take the case.

Twelve days later, on Monday night, July 15, 2019, the Fraternal Order of Police, Lodge #36, announced that O’Neill had resigned, effective immediately. Police Chief Scott Ruszkowski confirmed that he had received the resignation:

“I am in receipt of his resignation letter. I will not make any further comment at this time.” — Chief Scott Ruszkowski, July 2019

The union cited the stress of the shooting and its aftermath, the national media attention, and the family’s lawsuit. A crowdfunding effort for O’Neill raised nearly $80,000 (as reported by the Tribune).

On March 6, 2020, Special Prosecutor Hertel announced his determination and released his report the same day. His conclusion, in his own words:

“The use of deadly force by Sgt. O’Neill was justified based upon the threat and imminent danger presented by the sharp-edged knife in the right hand of Mr. Logan.” — Special Prosecutor Ric Hertel, March 6, 2020

Put in the legal frame: the state could not prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the force was not justified. No charge was filed in the shooting. That is the official end of the criminal question in Eric Logan’s death, and this file prints it exactly as the record holds it.

3 · THE OTHER CASE — WHAT THE AFFIDAVIT ALLEGED

This section is allegation. Everything in it comes from a probable-cause affidavit — the state’s sworn statement of what it believed in March 2020. An affidavit is not a finding. What O’Neill admitted, under oath, in his own plea is in Section 4. The two are printed separately because they are not the same thing.

The encounter the affidavit describes happened on May 16, 2019 — one month before the Logan shooting — near Calvert and Michigan streets. The affidavit states that O’Neill was in his police cruiser, in uniform, when he pulled up next to a woman and solicited her for a sex act; that he paid her $20; and the charging documents describe him as appearing “in a state of nudity” and having “fondled his genitals.” The woman told police she was “walking the streets, trying to make money.”

The allegation did not come from the woman. A fellow South Bend officer brought it forward in early July 2019; the department’s internal investigation followed, and its findings went to Special Prosecutor Hertel.

On March 6, 2020 — the same day he announced the shooting was justified — Hertel filed three charges: felony ghost employment, felony official misconduct, and misdemeanor public indecency. O’Neill was booked into the St. Joseph County Jail that morning and released on a promise to appear.

4 · THE PLEA AND THE SENTENCE

The plea agreement was filed on September 2, 2020. On September 8, 2020, before St. Joseph Superior Court Judge John Marnocha, Ryan O’Neill pleaded guilty to felony ghost employment. The official-misconduct and public-indecency counts were dropped under the agreement.

What he admitted was the ghost-employment charge itself: that he took city pay while engaged in activity that was, in his attorney’s words at the hearing, “purely personal, private and not related to your official duties.” The Tribune’s account of the plea states that the count alleged he gave a woman $20 and performed a sex act with her while on duty one morning in May 2019. Indiana’s ghost-employment statute covers a public employee who accepts pay from a government entity for duties not related to the job — that is the crime he now stands convicted of.

On October 27, 2020, he was sentenced: two years’ probation, no jail. The terms allowed the probation to end early after one year with no violations. At the sentencing he read an apology. His own words for what he had done:

“I engaged in an unseemly act that was for my benefit alone.” — Ryan O’Neill, at sentencing, October 27, 2020 (as reported by the South Bend Tribune)

One term of the agreement outlasts the probation. Under the plea agreement, he agreed not to seek or accept any public employment, including as a police officer. No end date for that bar appears in any contemporaneous account of the agreement — not in the plea reporting, not in the sentencing reporting. The exact term is written in the plea agreement and the sentencing order themselves, and those documents are demanded in this file (Section 8). Until they are produced, this file prints the bar exactly as the agreement was reported: no public employment, no police work, no date attached that the public record can prove.

5 · THE FEDERAL LAWSUIT

Ten days after the shooting, on June 26, 2019, Eric Logan’s estate sued the City of South Bend and O’Neill in federal court — Estate of Eric Jack Logan v. City of South Bend, Indiana, and Ryan O’Neill, No. 3:19-cv-495 (N.D. Ind.) — alleging that the shooting was excessive deadly force in violation of the Fourth and Fourteenth Amendments. Attorney Pete Agostino represented the city and O’Neill; his own file in this series is File No. 4. O’Neill’s recorded Metro Homicide interview became a filing in the case, and the union, FOP Lodge #36, was subpoenaed in discovery.

On September 29, 2021, the district court granted summary judgment to O’Neill and the city (Document 161, published at 564 F. Supp. 3d 719). On October 7, 2022, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit affirmed (50 F.4th 614). The estate had argued that a jury could doubt O’Neill’s account because his body camera stayed off until after the shots, and because he had since been convicted of a felony. The court answered that the case must be decided on the record that exists — O’Neill the only surviving witness, with no physical evidence contradicting him — and that this record “compels a decision for O’Neill.”

That is the disposition, printed exactly: summary judgment for the defense in 2021, affirmed in 2022. A ratings website reports the case as “dismissed” on a different date in September 2021 with the court “ruling he acted reasonably.” It was neither. The docket and the two opinions are the record, and they are demanded in full in Section 8.

6 · THE RECORD BEFORE — 2008 AND WHAT IT LEFT BEHIND

Eleven years before the shooting, on July 11, 2008, Lt. Dave Newton filed an officer’s report voicing concern over “inappropriate racial remarks” by patrol officer O’Neill. Newton filed it on behalf of trainee Kelly Hibbs — O’Neill was Hibbs’s field training officer — who, Newton wrote, feared retaliation for speaking. The report’s existence is not a rumor: Prosecutor Cotter’s 2019 court petition quotes it, which is why a special prosecutor had to be appointed at all.

Hibbs’s account, as it surfaced in federal court records: from the patrol car, spotting a Black woman, O’Neill asked, “do you want to get some of that black meat?” Passing a Black man walking with a white woman: “man I hate seeing that, it makes me sick, that makes me want to throw up.” Newton also relayed another officer’s account of derogatory comments about Muslim and Arabic people at a breakfast stop. Those words are printed here as what they are — one trainee’s account, carried in a lieutenant’s report, never sustained. About a month later Newton filed a second memo defending himself against potential retaliation.

The department’s answer is also in the record, and it is printed with the same weight: the internal-affairs investigation found Newton’s assertion “not sustained” — stated publicly in 2019 by the department’s spokesman, Ken Garcia, and by Mayor Pete Buttigieg.

The rest of the thread came out under oath. In a deposition in the federal litigation, Chief Scott Ruszkowski acknowledged that he was aware of the prior allegations, and testified that O’Neill had been removed as a field training officer over them. The department promoted O’Neill to sergeant in 2015, after he passed a polygraph examination on the allegation — a promotion that came the year before Ruszkowski’s tenure as chief began, in March 2016, and that this file attributes to the department that made it.

The rest of the personnel picture, as reported: the Tribune counted nine formal complaints against him during his tenure, three of them substantiated after internal review. The New York Times, reading his personnel file, found one minor disciplinary action — a one-day suspension in 2004 for driving his police car into a concrete pole. And in 2008, two Black arrestees sued him in federal court alleging excessive force and a racial slur; both suits were dismissed for failure to pay the filing fees, and no court ever reached their merits. In December 2019, six months after a shooting no camera recorded, the Board of Public Safety approved changes to the department’s body-camera policy.

7 · IS HE STILL A POLICE OFFICER?

No — and the record closes that question from four directions at once.

He resigned on July 15, 2019, effective immediately, nineteen years into the job. He is a convicted felon: the ghost-employment plea of September 8, 2020, stands. His own plea agreement bars him from public employment, including as a police officer. And Indiana’s decertification statute, IC 5-2-1-12.5, makes a felony conviction a ground for the Law Enforcement Training Board to revoke an officer’s basic certificate outright.

Two honest limits, printed because this series prints them. First: when the training board’s published decertification list — every certificate the board has revoked — was read in full for this file on November 4, 2026, Ryan O’Neill’s name was not on it. Whatever the state did or did not do with his certificate, it is not on the public list of revocations; his individual certification record is demanded in Section 8. Second: no public-pay record for him after 2019 was located in the state’s compensation records as searched for this file, and no reporting from 2020 through today places him in any police, sheriff’s, campus or security job. If a record exists that puts him back in uniform anywhere, it has not surfaced — and if it surfaces, it will be added to this file at this address.

On the public record, his policing ended the night the union announced his resignation — July 15, 2019. Everything since has been paper: a clearance, a charge, a plea, a sentence, and two courts ruling in his favor on the shooting. The badge never came back.

THE QUESTIONS THE RECORD LEAVES OPEN

Put the whole file together and the open questions are short, and every one of them is a document. How long does the plea agreement’s bar on public employment actually run — the one number no public account of his case has ever printed? Under what cause number does his criminal case sit in the Superior Court, that no docket search located? What did the state do with his certificate after a felony conviction, when his name appears on no revocation list? And what does Special Prosecutor Hertel’s full report say, beyond the one paragraph the public has read?

What is not in question is the shape of the record itself. A sergeant shot a man in a dark parking lot with his camera off. The system he worked for cleared the shooting, in writing, and convicted him of a felony for the month before it. He resigned, he pleaded, he apologized, and the courts closed the civil case in his favor. Every word of that is on paper — and the paper is still coming.

This file is a living file. When the records land — the plea agreement, the sentencing order, the affidavit, the full Hertel report, the certification record — they will be added here, at this address, whichever way they cut.
