This file is built from the government's own records: court orders, appellate opinions, docket pulls, her committee's own campaign filings, certified state payroll reports, and letters her office signed. Where a court has made a finding, this file prints the finding. Where a claim exists only as an allegation in a complaint or a motion, this file labels it an allegation, every time. Where the record has not been produced yet, this file says so and names the pull that settles it.

It also prints what the record shows in her favor, in its own section, in full. No court has ever found that Vicki Becker lied, knowingly withheld evidence, or committed misconduct, and this file will not say otherwise. What the courts have found, case by case, is below.

§01 — The Office

Vicki Elaine Becker has run the Elkhart County Prosecutor's Office since January 9, 2017. She was not first elected to it in a public vote: a Republican caucus selected her 72–22 to succeed Curtis T. Hill Jr. when Hill became Indiana Attorney General, after fourteen years as his Chief Deputy Prosecuting Attorney (Indiana Prosecuting Attorneys Council biography; The Indiana Lawyer, December 2016). She was elected to full terms in 2018 and 2022, and in both 2022 and 2026 no opponent filed against her. On November 3, 2026, Election Day, she was the only name on the ballot for her office; this file will be updated with the certified result.

She was admitted to the Indiana bar October 21, 1997 (Roll of Attorneys No. 19988-49). Her Roll of Attorneys entry states, verbatim, that she has "no disciplinary history." She chaired the Indiana Prosecuting Attorneys Council in 2023–24.

The Seabolt / Dillard Removal — The Court's Own Words
"The Elkhart County Prosecutor's Office has a special interest in the outcome… because Ms. Becker is a witness in post-conviction, the entire Office should be removed so as not to taint any particular prosecutor for the cross-examination of the elected Prosecutor… the appearance of impropriety and the need for transparency in the instant proceedings in the best interests of the Elkhart community outweigh any interest in Ms. Becker serving."

§03 — Seabolt: The Signature

Iris Seabolt was charged in 2003 with the August 2000 killing of A.J. Williams. Her trial began in September 2004; she pleaded guilty mid-trial after the State rested and was sentenced to 45 years. She was paroled on May 5, 2022, after nearly nineteen years. The State's case had no forensic evidence and hinged on jailhouse informants developed by Elkhart Detective Stephen Rezutko.

From The Court's Order — ¶61, ¶169, ¶170
The State's December 24, 2003 discovery letter was "from Denise Robinson (signed by Vicki Becker)." The State's discovery disclosures "included neither the 1996 internal affairs report" that disciplined Detective Rezutko for conduct unbecoming an officer, "nor the 2001 internal affairs report" that sustained a finding of malfeasance against him. Testifying in February 2026, "Prosecutor Becker confirmed that the State's discovery disclosures did not include either the 1996 or 2001 EPD internal affairs reports."
Seabolt post-conviction order (20D03-2106-PC-000019), ¶¶61, 169, 170.

The same order carries the counterweight, and this file prints it: lead prosecutor Denise Robinson testified she had no knowledge of the Rezutko internal-affairs investigations and would have disclosed them if she had; another prosecutor testified the files were Giglio material that should have been disclosed and that Elkhart prosecutors did not have free access to police personnel files at the time. The court's findings of a "blatant violation" of Seabolt's due-process rights run against the State. No court found that Becker personally knew of the reports in 2003.

The outcome is not in dispute: the court vacated the conviction in April 2026, and on September 2, 2026 the case was dismissed. Iris Seabolt is fully exonerated, Elkhart County's eighth exoneration since 1989.

§04 — Royer and Canen: The Case She Personally Tried

The Indiana Court of Appeals recites the fact plainly: then-Chief Deputy Prosecutor Vicki Becker was "primarily responsible for initiating charges against Royer and Canen" and tried the case for the State (State v. Royer, 20A-PC-955, April 8, 2021). Andrew Royer and Lana Canen were convicted in 2005 of the 2002 strangulation of Helen Sailor, 94. The convictions unraveled on three documented failures:

The fingerprint. The State's most important evidence was Detective Dennis Chapman's latent-print identification of Canen. Chapman was not qualified to make it, later admitted the print was not Canen's, and was disciplined and retired. The post-conviction court found both halves of Becker's part, and this file prints both: Chapman "misled Ms. Becker into believing that he was qualified," she was never given his résumé, and, in the same finding, "pursuant to Rule 3.8, Brady v. Maryland, and Giglio v. United States, Ms. Becker should have disclosed such exculpatory and impeachment evidence to the defense." Becker stipulated she would not have recommended charging Canen without Chapman's identification.

The detective. Lead detective Carlton Conway was removed from the homicide unit before trial over supervisors' fears about his misrepresentations. None of it was disclosed. The Court of Appeals found Conway committed perjury at trial and called his false testimony "particularly galling." The opinion does not find Becker knew of the removal.

The witness. Key witness Nina Porter was promised consideration and paid $2,000, undisclosed. The post-conviction court called it "a classic Giglio violation." She later recanted.

Canen was exonerated in 2012. Royer's new trial was granted in 2020 and affirmed in 2021, the Court of Appeals writing: "Simply put, Royer did not receive a fair criminal trial." On June 30, 2021, Becker herself moved to dismiss the murder charge, citing "the finite resources of the State of Indiana," and Royer went free. The City of Elkhart settled with Royer for $11,725,000, the largest wrongful-conviction settlement in Indiana history. Across the four Elkhart exoneration settlements (Cooper, Parish, Royer, Sims), the total is $26,625,000.

§05 — Alexander and Brena: Two Stories About One Gun

On August 13, 2003, Karla Castro, 20, was killed in an Elkhart drive-by shooting. The State's theory throughout: Ignacio "Nacho" Bahena fired from an SUV driven by Rodolfo Alexander. The gun came from Eduardo Brena. On what happened to that gun, the record holds two sworn stories, and Becker prosecuted both.

2004, Brena's plea: Brena pleaded guilty to providing the gun to Bahena on the day of the shooting. Becker represented the State; asked if she wanted the court to accept the plea, she answered, "Yes, I do, your Honor." At sentencing she said Brena "recklessly provided that handgun" and "enabled" the shooting. Brena received six years.

2005, Alexander's trial: Becker was again the prosecutor. She called Brena, who now testified he gave the gun to Alexander a couple of days before the shooting, and that Alexander passed it to Bahena. Alexander was convicted of murder as an accomplice and sentenced to 55 years.

Allegation — Labelled As Such

Alexander's March 2024 post-conviction motion alleges Becker knowingly presented false testimony and advanced two contradictory gun-transfer theories to convict two men, and asked that she and her office be disqualified because she is expected to be a central witness. That is a defense allegation in a pending case. No court has adopted it. What the docket shows: a subpoena as to Becker was quashed May 7, 2024; the disqualification petition was denied November 15, 2024; a post-conviction order issued December 19, 2025 against Alexander; and an appeal, No. 25A-PC-03259, is pending. The text of those orders has been requested from the Elkhart County Clerk and has not yet been produced.

Her only public response, by email to ProPublica in April 2024: "As this is an actively pending matter, I am not able to engage in an interview of any kind regarding the case, nor may a representative of the State. All information should come from observing the public proceedings at this time. Thank you for reaching out."

§06 — Dillard, Cooper, Parish, Sims: The Institutional Record

Reginald Dillard was convicted in 2000 on a case built on jailhouse informants developed by Detective Rezutko, with no physical evidence. The trial prosecutors were Michael Christofeno and Curtis Hill; no source places Becker on that trial team, and this file does not either. What touches her office is the end: her office was removed from his post-conviction case in June 2025 (§02), the special prosecutor agreed a Brady violation required vacatur, and on February 23, 2026 Dillard was dismissed with prejudice and released, exonerated after 27 years. His October 2026 civil suit does not name Becker as a defendant.

Keith Cooper received Indiana's first innocence-based pardon in February 2017. Becker's role was institutional and post-conviction: reporting by ProPublica and the South Bend Tribune describes her, at a Cooper hearing, attacking former prosecutor Christofeno over his letter supporting the pardon. Cooper's settlement: $7,500,000. Christopher Parish, from the same investigation, was exonerated in 2005; his prosecutor-era figure was a deputy, not Becker. Mack Sims ($2,400,000 settlement) predates her Elkhart service entirely.

§07 — Eggleston: The Open Docket

Brent Eggleston was convicted in July 2003 of felony murder in the November 2002 killing of Clayton Schwab and received 60 years. In July 2025, the Notre Dame Exoneration Justice Clinic filed his post-conviction petition (20C01-2507-PC-000017), alleging newly discovered evidence of non-involvement, withheld favorable evidence, and ineffective assistance of counsel. Those are the petition's allegations, printed as allegations.

A claim circulates that Becker was removed from the Eggleston case and a special prosecutor appointed. As of this file's publication, no record produced anywhere documents any removal, any special prosecutor, or any order of any kind in that case. The clinic's own case page describes only the July 2025 filing. The docket pull that settles it has been outstanding since November 1, 2026. When it lands, this section is updated, whichever way it cuts. Until then, the removal claim is an allegation, and this file prints it as one.

No source in this file documents Becker having any role in Eggleston's 2003 trial, and none is claimed here.

§08 — The Civil Suits That Name Her

Dunn v. City of Elkhart (3:24-cv-00601, filed July 25, 2024). DeWayne Dunn was convicted in 2011, in a trial Becker prosecuted, of the 2008 death of Angel Torres, and was later exonerated after more than twelve years. His federal complaint names "Vicki E. Becker" personally and alleges prosecutorial misconduct, including an allegation that she enlisted a pathologist to falsify an autopsy report. That is a pending complaint's allegation. No court has found it. The suit is pending with no disposition against her.

Royer v. City of Elkhart (3:22-cv-00254). The complaint names Dennis Chapman and Vicki E. Becker in their individual capacities, alleging presence at and encouragement of a coercive interrogation and a conspiracy concerning the fingerprint evaluation. Allegations, labelled as such. What the court record shows (December 13, 2022): the Prosecutor's Office and the State of Indiana were dismissed entirely; two counts were allowed to proceed against the individual defendants; the emotional-distress count was dismissed as to Becker; the $11,725,000 settlement was with the City and was partial. The final disposition of the claims against Becker personally is not in any record produced to date; the current federal docket pull is outstanding, and this section updates when it lands.

§09 — The Suits Her Side Won

Phillips v. City of Elkhart (3:24-cv-566): dismissed in full on June 30, 2025. The suit grew out of Becker's October 28, 2022 letter to the Elkhart police chief advising that supervisors' testimony would lead a reasonable person to believe Detective Joy Phillips has "a reputation for dishonesty" and that her credibility must be disclosed to the defense. The letter's existence and date are documented through the litigation; the letter itself has never been produced in response to a public-records request (see §14). Graham v. City of Elkhart (3:21-cv-495): the federal claims against Becker were dismissed January 24, 2022. In Burkins (2023), an appellate decision affirmatively found no prosecutorial misconduct in her office's handling of that case.

§10 — The Decisions File

The Jayco cats (2025). On February 27, 2025, three Jayco employees were accused of placing two live cats in a trash compactor at the Middlebury facility. On June 9, 2025, Becker's office filed Level 6 felony animal-cruelty charges against all three (20D03-2506-F6-000624/625/626), then dismissed all three within days, in part over a clerical error in the probable-cause affidavit. On June 25, 2025, the office announced in writing it would not refile, reasoning that Indiana trapping law and the property-damage exception allow a property owner to dispose of a trapped animal and that the cats' suffering was not prolonged. On September 23, 2025, Alley Cat Allies stated it had obtained a forensic veterinary opinion that the cats "would have suffered severe and prolonged pain," and asked the Attorney General to appoint a special prosecutor. No appointment was made. The veterinary opinion exists as that organization's statement; it is printed here as such.

Thomas Ricke (2025). Thomas Ricke, 39, died of dehydration in the Elkhart County jail on May 11, 2025. The county Homicide Unit investigated; Becker's office closed the criminal case with no charges. The entire public record of that closure is one attributed sentence in local reporting. His family sued the Sheriff's Office and the medical provider in September 2026; Becker is not a defendant. No declination memo or statement of her office's reasoning has been produced.

The office she runs. By her own July 2023 statement, the office ran 18 attorneys against 31 authorized positions; the county granted six additional positions in 2022 and she filled none.

§11 — Bradberry and Yoder: The Credibility File

James L. Bradberry was Captain of the Sheriff's Administrative Division, supervising the civilian process servers. When three of those servers were indicted in 2018 for roughly 2,500 unworked hours and about $45,000 in unearned pay, Bradberry, who had testified to the grand jury, changed his story afterward; court documents record his admission that he had known the information "for quite some time" and did not disclose it. He resigned March 2, 2019. Becker's office charged him March 5, 2019 with three counts of false informing, not perjury, and no perjury charge was ever filed. In September 2019 he pleaded guilty to one count; the other two were dismissed; sentence: one year suspended, 30 hours of community service, a $100 fine, probation. The three servers each pleaded guilty to criminal conversion.

Certified Indiana Gateway payroll reports then put him back on the county payroll: "Bradberry, James L — Court Security Officer — $88,624.86 (2025)." The reports show a five-year gap between his 2019 resignation and his 2024 return, so any claim he was immediately rehired is contradicted by the county's own numbers. Who hired him back, when exactly, and who approved it are not in any record produced yet; the Sheriff's Office production is outstanding (§14). His 2019 case no longer appears on the public court portal, which is consistent with an expungement but does not prove one. Any claim that Becker's office helped or consented to an expungement is an allegation; her office held a statutory notice seat in expungement proceedings, and no petition or order naming her office's position has been produced.

Justin Ray Yoder was a Ligonier city police officer working as a school resource officer in 2002 when he pleaded guilty in Noble County (57D02-0207-CM-000685) to contributing to the delinquency of a minor and furnishing alcohol to a minor, after admitting he provided beer to teenagers, 19 and 17, at his home. The court's disposition text bars him from employment "in any law enforcement or criminal justice system," and that bar sits inside the one-year probation term in the text produced; it is not printed anywhere as a lifetime ban, and the signed sentencing order that settles its legal character is outstanding from the Noble County Clerk. By 2015 he was an Elkhart County Sheriff's deputy, hired in the prior sheriff's era; certified Gateway reports list "Yoder, Justin R — Sheriff PATROL OFFICER — $91,367.90 (2025)," working as a K9 handler. His own later expungement petition, which is in hand, misstates his arrest date by three years and omits one of his two convictions.

Becker has no role in a Noble County expungement as a matter of law; notice rights there belong to the Noble County prosecutor. What is hers is the question this file ends on: whether her office carries credibility (Giglio) designations for officers whose records her own office's files touch. That answer rides on the production described in §14.

§12 — The Money: Her Own Filings

From her committee's own Clerk-stamped campaign reports (Committee to Elect Vicki Becker for Prosecutor): career fundraising of $49,552.70, essentially all of it in 2018. The largest donor is Becker herself, $16,748.70. The sole organizational donor of size is ERW Transport, LLC, $10,000 on February 20, 2018. No case, contract, charge, or office connection between ERW Transport and her office has been found anywhere, and no motive for the contribution is documented; this file prints the contribution and the absence, and nothing more. No PACs, no bondsmen, and no law firms appear anywhere in her record.

The filings themselves carry discrepancies, printed here as filing facts: a $3,814.70 debt her committee owed to Becker herself at the end of 2018 disappears in the 2019 annual report with no repayment, forgiveness, or contribution entry; a $9,813 pre-primary debt likewise vanishes; the 2022 annual report was filed January 27, 2023, nine days after the deadline, with the treasurer's certification unsigned, then amended January 30. Her Statements of Economic Interests, which by law file with the Indiana Commission on Judicial Qualifications, are not published online; they were requested October 31, 2026 and are outstanding.

§13 — The Counterweight, Printed Straight

In October 2026, Becker was appointed special prosecutor in the Tippecanoe County investigation of State Senator Ron Alting, arising from six alleged self-checkout thefts at Lafayette Walmarts. No charges had been filed as of the last reporting in this file, and Alting announced October 21, 2026 that he will resign if re-elected; he had not resigned at last report. The appointment is a documented fact and it cuts in her favor: the same court system that removed her office from Seabolt, Dillard, and Taylor handed the elected prosecutor from the next county a sitting senator's file. This file prints it without inflating it and without burying it.

§14 — The Records Fight

Her Signature — October 28, 2026

A public-records request for her office's Brady/Giglio material was denied in full in one day, in a letter signed by Becker herself. The denial carries one substantive admission, in her office's own words: for Brady/Giglio designations, "no written policy was in existence in 2022."

Elkhart County Prosecutor's Office APRA denial, October 28, 2026.

Its acknowledgment was due by November 7, 2026. Three requests to the Elkhart County Clerk (candidate records, the certified Seabolt and Dillard orders, and the Bradberry file) were all answered the same way on November 2, 2026: complete the Clerk's own "Request for Public Record" form first. This file is a living file. Each production, each docket pull, and each answer lands here as a dated update at this same address.

§15 — What The Record Shows In Her Favor

1. No disciplinary history, per the Roll of Attorneys' own words. No state audit finding, no criminal charge, and no civil liability finding against her exists in this record.
2. She dismissed the Royer case herself in 2021 rather than retrying a man whose trial the Court of Appeals had ruled unfair.
3. The Seabolt court credited her sworn testimony that she would strive for objectivity, and removed her on appearance grounds anyway. Both halves are in §02.
4. The lead Seabolt prosecutor and another prosecutor both corroborated under oath that they did not know of the Rezutko files either; the Seabolt findings run against the State, not against Becker personally.
5. The Phillips suit against her office's designation was dismissed in full; the Graham claims were dismissed; the Alexander disqualification was denied.
6. An appellate court affirmatively found no prosecutorial misconduct by her office in Burkins (2023).
7. The fingerprint examiner misled her, on the post-conviction court's own finding (§04).
8. She was appointed special prosecutor in a sitting state senator's investigation (§13).
9. No opponent has filed against her in two straight cycles; she took 11,582 votes in the May 2026 primary.
