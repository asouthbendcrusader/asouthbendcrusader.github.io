What this file is. This file is built out of the lodge’s own federal tax returns, City of Mishawaka council minutes and ordinances, contemporaneous reporting in the South Bend Tribune and on WNDU, and the lodge’s own public Facebook page — photographed above, and exhibited in Section 7 — and nothing else. Every factual statement here is tied to the document or the named report it came from. This series publishes only what the paper says, and says plainly what the paper does not.
What this file does not claim. Read this first. Nothing in this file accuses the lodge or any officer of a crime. The lodge’s charitable-gaming operation ended years ago, and this file states that plainly: an ended operation is not a theft, and nothing in the returns says otherwise. Where a number in the lodge’s own filings is unexplained, it is printed as an open question the lodge can answer from its own records — because it is one. Where a fact could not be found, this file says NOT FOUND instead of guessing.

1 · WHAT THE LODGE IS

The organization. Fraternal Order of Police, Lodge 91, Mishawaka — the local lodge of the national police fraternity, and the recognized bargaining agent for Mishawaka police officers: the City of Mishawaka negotiates its police contract with this lodge. Its federal tax identity is EIN 23-7585999, a 501(c)(8) fraternal beneficiary society holding its exemption under an IRS ruling of November 1972, as a subordinate under the Fraternal Order of Police group exemption.

The hall. The lodge sits at 1825 E. 12th Street, Mishawaka, Indiana 46544 (mailing address: PO Box 292, Mishawaka, IN 46546), telephone 574-258-9155. The building is a 15,000-square-foot hall that the lodge markets to the public as a wedding and event venue — directory listings bill it as the largest reception availability in Mishawaka. Its website, mishawakafop.com, solicits donations under a “Donate Today” banner and describes the lodge’s mission as “bridging the gap between our officers and the citizens and businesses of Mishawaka.” The lodge also speaks to the public through a Facebook page and a Twitter account, @MishawakaFOP91. What the lodge says and does through those public accounts is part of its public record — see Section 7.

The page’s manager. The lodge’s Facebook page states who runs it, in its own bio line, printed here verbatim: “Comment and Posts are current managed by Brother Rich Freeman.” (The lodge’s Facebook page, as screenshotted November 5, 2026; the original screenshot is preserved in this file’s research pack.) “Brother Rich Freeman” is the lodge’s president, Richard “Rich” Freeman, in the officer table below.

A decade of deficits. The lodge spent more than it took in for ten of the eleven years from 2013 through 2023 — only 2013 closed with a surplus. Over the same span, revenue fell from $476,467 to $20,981, a decline of roughly 95 percent. Cumulative operating losses for 2014 through 2023 run to about $482,000.

The gaming money — and the year it stopped

The engine under the old revenue was charitable gaming. The lodge’s 2018 Form 990 reports gaming that grossed $1,014,241 and netted $76,149 after $938,092 in direct expenses. The filings for 2012 through 2017 show gaming grossing between $1 million and $4 million a year. From the 2019 return onward, gaming does not appear in the filings at all — and the lodge’s unrelated-business-income indicator flips from “Y” (2012–2018) to “N” (2019 onward), tracking the same change. The gaming operation ended. Nothing in any return indicates that gaming money went anywhere it should not have gone, and this file makes no such claim.

After gaming ended, the remaining revenue lines were smaller. Hall rentals netted roughly $56,000–$59,000 a year in 2018 and 2019. Contributions were $19,523 in 2023. The 2023 return also reports fundraising at a loss: $11,413 spent on fundraising to raise $5,790 gross.

The debt, and what the officers are paid

The lodge’s balance sheet has been shrinking its one clear obligation: the secured mortgage balance on the hall fell from about $156,000 in 2018 to about $87,000 in 2023 (total liabilities, per the returns: $155,863 in 2018; $87,053 in 2023). As for pay: the returns show officer compensation at essentially $0 across the decade, with a $1,200 figure appearing for two officers in the 2022 summary data. The balance sheet across the same years shows reserves drawn down as revenue fell.

A number that is not true. Nonprofit-aggregator websites list a multi-million-dollar “total income” figure for Lodge 91. That figure is wrong — a mislabeled column in an aggregator’s table, repeated from site to site. The lodge’s real peak revenue, per its own Form 990, is $476,467 (2013). Anyone quoting a multi-million-dollar income figure for this lodge, for it or against it, is quoting an error. This file does not.

The lodge has also filed a Form 990 for fiscal 2024 and a Form 990-EZ for fiscal 2025, per the Cause IQ index of IRS filings. Those figures join this file when the returns themselves are in hand.

3 · THE 2020 QUESTION

One line in the lodge’s own balance sheet does not explain itself, and this file prints it as what it is: a question.

In 2019, the lodge’s Form 990 reported year-end assets of $268,527. In 2020 — a year the lodge took in $25,360, spent $59,334, and ran a $33,974 deficit, while its liabilities fell by about $25,536 — the return reported year-end assets of $590,752. The assets rose $322,225 in a deficit year. Arithmetically, the 2020 return requires roughly $381,735 in “other changes in net assets” (Form 990, Part XI, line 9) beyond its operating result and its debt movement.

There are innocent instruments that produce a line like that: a property revaluation, a restatement, relief funds, a filing correction. Any one of them could be the whole story. The explanation, if one was filed, sits in the narrative schedule to the lodge’s own 2020 return — its Schedule O — which has not been produced in the public indexes searched. So the question stands exactly where the lodge’s own numbers leave it: $322,225 appeared on the lodge’s balance sheet in 2020, and the lodge’s public filings do not say from where. The lodge can close this question with one page of its own paperwork. Until that page is public, the question stays in this file, dated and open.

4 · THE LABOR FILE — THE DEAL THAT CUT FIFTEEN POSITIONS

Late 2023. The City of Mishawaka, the police department’s leadership, and Lodge 91 jointly designed a move to 12-hour shifts for Mishawaka officers. Lodge President Freeman brought the proposal to the administration, and Mayor Dave Wood publicly commended “the FOP leaders” for the collaboration. Inside the same deal, the city cut the department’s budgeted officer positions from 114 to 99 — fifteen positions erased from the budget — and called the model “revenue neutral,” because the fifteen-position difference simply went unfunded. (City of Mishawaka published accounts of the shift approval and the Mayor’s November communicator letter.)

The documented facts of the deal are these: the lodge co-designed a staffing plan with the city; the plan reduced the department’s budgeted positions by fifteen; and the city presented the plan as revenue neutral because the fifteen positions went unfunded. Nothing in that arrangement is illegal, and this file does not call it anything else. Whether the cut reduced actual police coverage is not answered by the documents in hand. The staffing records that could answer it are requested in Section 8.

October 2025. The Mishawaka Common Council held executive sessions on October 9, October 13, and October 24, 2025, noticed as sessions on “FOP Strategy Related to Collective Bargaining” under IC 5-14-1.5-6.1(b)(2)(A) — the city planning, behind closed doors, how to negotiate with the lodge.

January 5, 2026. The Council passed Ordinance 2026-02, approving a one-year collective bargaining agreement with Lodge 91. The 2026 agreement added specialty pay — including drone-operator pay — along with light-duty language, detective-bureau on-call overtime rules, training rules, and a personal-day buyout. President Freeman spoke in favor of the agreement at the meeting. (Mishawaka Common Council minutes, January 5, 2026.) The agreement runs one year, so the bargaining — and the executive sessions that precede it — recur annually.

5 · 2014 — “BREATHE EASY”

December 2014. The Notre Dame women’s basketball team wore “I Can’t Breathe” shirts before a game, invoking the death of Eric Garner. Mishawaka police officer Jason Barthel began selling shirts reading “Breathe Easy” and “Don’t Break the Law,” printed with a police badge over a thin blue line, at South Bend Uniform — the uniform store Barthel owned. (Associated Press, December 2014.)

Three members of the South Bend Common Council — Oliver Davis, Henry Davis Jr., and Valerie Schey — publicly called on Barthel to stop selling the shirts, calling the message divisive. The South Bend Tribune put a number under the story: the City of South Bend was a frequent customer of Barthel’s store, with more than $64,700 in city receipts to South Bend Uniform since January 2013 — a neighboring city’s uniform money flowing to a store owned by an active Mishawaka officer.

The lodge’s part in the affair is its president’s. Lodge 91 President Pasquale Rulli attended the meeting between the Notre Dame team and local police-union representatives, and afterward told the media that all parties had agreed to avoid speaking further about the matter in public.

Officer Barthel today — the whole of the documented record. On December 16, 2025, WNDU reported that Officer Barthel had been placed on paid administrative leave amid an internal investigation. The department gave no reason. That report is everything the public record currently says about his status, and it is all this file prints about it. Anything beyond that report is not in the record, and what is not in the record is not in this file.

The procurement thread underneath the 2014 story was never fully pulled: how much public money, in total, has flowed to an active officer’s private store, and under what purchasing rules. The City of South Bend’s own purchase records for 2013–2016 are demanded in Section 8.

6 · VILLAGE GREEN — THE SCHOCK QUESTION

January 31, 2022. At the Village Green Mobile Home Park in Mishawaka, Officer Garrett Schock fatally shot Michael Haas. In February 2022, the St. Joseph County Prosecutor ruled the shooting a justified homicide. That ruling is printed here exactly as it stands: the shooting was ruled justified, the criminal matter is closed, and nothing in this file reopens it. The South Bend Tribune later obtained the department’s video of the shooting through a public-records request (March 2022).

What remains open is a question about the lodge, not about the shooting. Whether Lodge 91 provided legal-defense fund support, financial disbursements, or other material support to Officer Schock is, on the record searched, unknown. No public statement by the lodge on the shooting was located in this research. The question was put in writing to the Mishawaka Police Department’s Chief’s Office on October 11, 2026 (Section 8). When the answer arrives, it will be added to this file at this address.

9 · WHAT THIS FILE DOES NOT KNOW

The limits of this file, stated plainly.

The 2020 Schedule O. The $322,225 asset increase on the lodge’s 2020 return (Section 3) is unexplained on the face of the public filings. The narrative schedule that would explain it has not been produced in the indexes searched. OPEN.

The Schock defense question. Whether the lodge funded or otherwise supported Officer Schock’s defense after the Village Green shooting is unknown; the question was asked in writing on October 11, 2026. OPEN.

The PAC question. No Lodge 91 political action committee was located in the Indiana campaign-finance records searched. The state’s official portal blocked automated checks, so that negative result is provisional, and the search continues. The Indiana State FOP’s own PAC and its telemarketing history belong to the state lodge, a separate entity — they are not Lodge 91’s record, and nothing in this file attributes them to Lodge 91.

The donation question. The lodge solicits donations on its own website. The summary-level filing data does not break out how donation money is spent — member benefit, charitable programs, or the hall. That breakdown sits in the full returns. OPEN.

The full rosters. Officer names and the financial table in this file rest on summary-level Form 990 data, cross-checked across three indexes, and on council minutes. The full filed returns — including the Part VII rosters — were not retrievable for every year in the October 2026 pulls, and the fiscal 2024 and 2025 returns have not yet been read. Corrections from the paper, when it arrives, will be entered as dated updates.
